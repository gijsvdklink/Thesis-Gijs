\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:Methodology}
% ===============================================================

To be able to solve a CR problem via RL, a simulation environment has to be designed, a learning algorithm has to be selected and implemented, and the resulting models have to be evaluated. This chapter covers the methodology of those three steps.

Section~\ref{sec:Environment} describes the set-up of the environment. Section~\ref{sec:learning algorithm} states which learning algorithm was used to train the models and on which hardware they were trained. Section~\ref{sec:Experiments} defines the key performance indicators and describes the three experiments conducted with them.

\section{Environment}
\label{sec:Environment}

The environment used for the experiments was custom-developed for this research and inspired by existing BS-Gym environments \cite{groot2024}.  Each aircraft is modeled as an A-320 and the dynamics are handled by BS, while the actions, observations, and rewards are defined according to the guidelines of the Gymnasium API, an open-source Python library used to develop and test reinforcement learning algorithms.

Subsection~\ref{subsec:Scenarios} explains how the aircraft and the airspaces are created. Subsection~\ref{subsec:Selection} explains how the single aircraft that the model advises is selected, based on an urgency score. Subsections~\ref{subsec:actions}, \ref{subsec:observations} and~\ref{subsec:rewards} then define the actions, observations and rewards. Finally, Subsection~\ref{subsec:action_response} covers the action-response delay distributions that have been implemented.

\subsection{Scenarios}
\label{subsec:Scenarios}

The scenarios define the key characteristics of each episode, a single simulation run. At the beginning of a new episode, an airspace is generated, and the aircraft with starting positions and destinations are created. A new scenario is generated for every episode, following the steps in Figure~\ref{fig:scenario_steps}, so that the models are never trained or tested on the same traffic twice.

The scenarios are developed exclusively in two dimensions to reduce the required training time. One fundamental design consideration is the choice between structured and free flight airspace. In structured airspace, aircraft follow predefined airways, whereas in free flight airspace, trajectories are unconstrained, generating a richer distribution of multi-aircraft conflicts. To avoid overfitting, where the model learns to solve problems only in specific airway structures rather than resolving conflicts in general, the more conflict-rich free flight airspace was selected. The traffic density and the number of aircraft vary between episodes, but both are kept constant within a single episode.
 

\subsection{Selection of the Focus Ship}
\label{subsec:Selection}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:recap_gap}, MARL is not suitable for an RL-based CR tool intended to advise ATCOs: it allows multiple actions to be selected at each time step, yet an ATCO is unlikely to issue heading instructions to several aircraft simultaneously. A single-agent RL approach was therefore chosen for this research, inspired by the work of \textcite{sui2025}. The core idea is an explicit ranking mechanism that scores the urgency of all predicted conflicts in the airspace and selects the most critical aircraft to be the focus ship for the RL agent, based on the highest urgency score (Section~\ref{subsubsec:urgency}).

\subsubsection{Urgency}
\label{subsubsec:urgency}
For a pair of aircraft $(i, j)$, the pairwise urgency $u_{ij}$ is calculated by
\begin{equation}
    u_{ij} =
    \begin{cases}
        1 + 9\left(1 - \dfrac{d}{d_\mathrm{sep}}\right),
            & d < d_\mathrm{sep}, \\[1.0em]
        \dfrac{t_\mathrm{warn} - t_\mathrm{los}}{t_\mathrm{warn}},
            & 0 \le t_\mathrm{los} \le t_\mathrm{warn}, \\[1.0em]
        0, & \text{otherwise,}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:urgency}
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the current distance between aircraft $i$ and $j$, $d_\mathrm{sep} = 5$~NM is the separation minimum, $t_\mathrm{warn} = 360$~s is the warning horizon, which aligns with the concept of Medium Term Conflict Detection (MTCD) \cite{caac2024mtcd}. The term $t_\mathrm{los}$ is the predicted time to loss of separation. The pairwise urgency is symmetric by definition, $u_{ij} = u_{ji}$, and the formulas used to compute $d_\mathrm{cpa}$ and $t_\mathrm{los}$ are given in Appendix~\ref{app:cpa}. Figure~\ref{fig:urgency_calculation} shows an urgency matrix $U$, with $U_{ij} = u_{ij}$, for a scenario with six aircraft.

The focus ship is determined from the urgency matrix $U$ as follows.
Because the pairwise urgency is symmetric ($u_{ij} = u_{ji}$), both aircraft of a
conflict pair carry the same pairwise score, so the most urgent conflict in the
sector is the pair with the highest maximum urgency $\max_j u_{ij}$. To decide
which of these two aircraft becomes the focus ship, the row sum
$U_i = \sum_j u_{ij}$ is used: the aircraft with the larger row
sum becomes the focus ship, as this indicates involvement in further conflicts beyond the
shared one. In Figure~\ref{fig:urgency_calculation}, for example, aircraft~10 has
the highest urgency ($U_{10} = 0.86 + 0.31 = 1.17$) and is therefore selected as
the focus ship. 

Changing the focus ship every time the urgency scores shift would make the
problem unstable and hinder learning. Therefore, once a focus ship is selected
it is retained until 25~s after its conflict has been resolved. Only when a highly
urgent conflict appears elsewhere in the sector does the focus ship change immediately. When any pair satisfies
$u_{ij} \geq 0.67$, corresponding to a predicted loss of
separation within ${\approx}120$~s, the focus ship changes immediately to one of the conflicting aircraft in this pair. When no aircraft are in conflict ($u_{ij} = 0\ \forall\, i,j$), the focus ship becomes the aircraft with the largest heading drift, denoted as $\Delta\psi$. This drift is defined as the angle between the aircraft's current heading and the shortest path to its destination, and is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_geometry}.


\subsection{Actions}
\label{subsec:actions}

At every time step the model selects one action for the focus ship, and that action is turned into an advisory: the instruction that is issued to the pilot. The advisory is always executed, but only once the pilot's action-response delay has elapsed (Subsection~\ref{subsec:action_response}), unless the model replaces it with a new advisory before that moment. The term action therefore refers to what the model selects, and advisory to what the pilot is told to fly. The action space is discrete and consists of ten different actions, each with an assigned cost, listed in Table~\ref{tab:actions}. Subsection~\ref{subsec:rewards} describes how these costs contribute to the final reward.
These actions fall into three categories: heading changes, speed changes, and special actions. A workload cost is assigned to each action based on its magnitude. For instance, a -60-degree turn is more expensive than a -30-degree turn; however, executing a -30-degree turn twice is more expensive than executing a -60-degree turn once. Heading changes are always defined with respect to the initial random heading assigned when the aircraft was spawned. Speed changes are generally cheaper than turns and are bounded between Mach 0.74 and Mach 0.82. The 'do nothing' option is completely free, as it requires no intervention. Finally, the 'return to route' action, which sets the heading deviation to $\Delta\psi = 0$, is assigned a low cost to encourage the model to guide aircraft back to their initial headings.

\subsection{Observations}
\label{subsec:observations}

The observation space is a 27-element vector (Table~\ref{tab:observation}) that encodes information about the focus ship and its neighbors. Designing this space was one of the most challenging aspects of the environment, as the literature currently lacks a standard convention for encoding observations in two-dimensional conflict resolution. Since this research aims to assess the effects of action-response delays in RL-based CR, which so far has only been done for ACAS Xa, the observation space was constructed to align with the frameworks of ACAS Xa and ACAS Xu, as detailed in \textcite{owen2019}.

For this observation space, a nearest-neighbor algorithm was implemented. In a simulation with 30 aircraft, not all traffic is likely to be relevant to the focus ship in order to avoid a LoS. Therefore, only the four most relevant aircraft are encoded in the observation space. Assuming the focus ship is denoted as aircraft $i$, any conflicting aircraft $j$ (where $u_{ij} > 0$) are placed first in the observation space. The remaining slots are then filled by the closest aircraft, determined by the distance parameter $\rho$, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:conflict_geometry}.

For the focus ship, the observation includes the amount of drift $\Delta\psi$, the current speed $v_\mathrm{own}$, the advised heading $h_\mathrm{cmd}$, and the advised airspeed $v_\mathrm{cmd}$. The advised heading and airspeed inform the model of the previously issued advisories. Together with $v_\mathrm{own}$ and $\Delta\psi$, they also indicate whether the aircraft has already executed this action. If $h_\mathrm{cmd} = \Delta\psi$ and $v_\mathrm{own} = v_\mathrm{cmd}$, the current track (speed and heading) is in line with the latest selected advisory. 

Additionally, \texttt{retn\_conf} is a binary variable set to~1 if returning to the route (i.e., setting $\Delta\psi = 0$) results in a predicted conflict within 360 seconds. The variable \texttt{pending} is another binary indicator, set to~1 if an issued instruction has not yet been executed. Finally, $t_\mathrm{wait}$ represents the elapsed time since that instruction was issued, clipped at 70 seconds. In summary, the observations for the focus ship encode essential information regarding its current track, previously issued advisories, the safety of returning to its route, and the execution status of pending advisories.

The information regarding the intruders includes the range to each aircraft ($\rho$), the bearing relative to the focus ship's heading ($\theta$), the relative heading ($\psi$), their airspeed, and the predicted time to LoS.

\subsection{Rewards}
\label{subsec:rewards}

The reward function is purely negative and penalizes the focus ship only for experiencing a Loss of Separation (LoS), for issuing action advisories, and for drifting from its initial heading:
%
\begin{equation}
    R = R_\mathrm{los} + R_\mathrm{drift} + R_\mathrm{work}.
    \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
%
Each term in Equation~\ref{eq:reward} is scaled by a specific weight. These weights were chosen empirically to balance conflict resolution, the frequency of issued actions, and route drift. Their values are listed in Table~\ref{tab:reward_weights}. 

The first term applies when there is a LoS anywhere in the simulation:
%
\begin{equation}
    R_\mathrm{los} = -w_\mathrm{los}\,\mathbb{1}[LoS],
    \label{eq:r_los}
\end{equation}
%
where the indicator function is defined as:
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{1}[LoS] = 
    \begin{cases} 
        1, & \text{if } d < d_\mathrm{sep} \text{ for any aircraft pair } \\[1.0em]
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
%
The second term introduces a drift penalty based on the heading deviation $\Delta\psi$:
%
\begin{equation}
    R_\mathrm{drift} = -w_\mathrm{drift}\,\bigl(1 - \cos(\Delta\psi)\bigr).
    \label{eq:r_drift}
\end{equation}
%
The third term represents the workload cost of the issued instruction, scaled by a workload weight:
%
\begin{equation}
    R_\mathrm{work} = -w_\mathrm{work}\,\mathrm{cost}(a),
    \label{eq:r_work}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathrm{cost}(a)$ is the base cost of action $a$, as defined in Table~\ref{tab:actions}. This penalty discourages the model from issuing unnecessary instructions.

\subsection{Action-Response Delays}
\label{subsec:action_response}
Several types of action-response delay distribution have been implemented, each parameterised with a mean delay of $15$~s, $30$~s, or $45$~s. The delays are explained below and visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:delay_distributions}.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Delay}: The delay is fixed, so every advisory is executed exactly at the specified time.
    \item \textbf{Geometric Delay}: This delay is inspired by the probabilistic pilot models used in ACAS, as discussed in \textcite{chryssanthacopoulos2011}. The geometric shape of the distribution comes from a probabilistic Markov chain, which is visualised and explained in Appendix~\ref{app:markov_chain}.
    \item \textbf{Lognormal Delay}: The delay is randomly drawn from a lognormal
    distribution, inspired by the distribution found in \textcite{wustenbecker2026}. Appendix~\ref{app:lognormal} explains how this distribution was generated.
    \item \textbf{No delay}: There is also an option to conduct experiments with no delays, meaning that every selected action is immediately executed.
\end{itemize}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{images/midterm/delay_distributions.pdf}
    \caption{Action-response delay distributions for the three response models}
    \label{fig:delay_distributions}
\end{figure}
When a new focus ship is selected and an initial advisory is issued, a delay value is
sampled from the active distribution. This value determines the time elapsed between the
issuance of the first advisory and its execution. For example, if the first advisory for a
new focus ship is issued at $t = 15$~s and a delay of $25$~s is sampled, the
action is scheduled for execution at $t = 40$~s. If the model selects a new action while
this delay is still counting down, the new instruction overwrites the previous one and
this new action is the one that will be executed at $t = 40$~s.

A key implementation detail concerns the time scales involved. BS updates the
simulation every $1$ simulation second, but the model only receives observations and
selects actions every $5$~s. The sampled delay is therefore evaluated against the
$1$~s time step of BS, so an advisory is executed at the first $1$~s step at which the sampled
delay has elapsed. The pseudocode below illustrates how the environment is propagated.
\begin{lstlisting}[style=pyPseudo, language=Python]
while episode not done:
    action = model(obs)              # new action selected every 5 s
    env.queue(action, delay)         # action is queued with its sampled delay
    for t in range(5):
        if response_delay_elapsed(t):
            bluesky.execute(action)  # action executed once the delay has elapsed
        bluesky.step(dt=1)           # BlueSky propagates every 1 s
    obs = env.observe()              # new observation every 5 s
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Learning Algorithm}
\label{sec:learning algorithm}
All models were trained with the learning algorithm PPO \parencite{DBLP:journals/corr/SchulmanWDRK17}, using the
implementation provided by Stable Baselines 3 (SB3) \parencite{sb3_algos}. PPO was chosen as the learning algorithm because it is currently one of the most widely used policy-gradient algorithms in both research and industry, it is accessible through open-source platforms such as SB3, and it supports discrete action spaces.

The experiments were run on the FLORIS2 server of NLR. This machine is equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 5317 processor with 12 cores and 24 threads, a base clock of 3.0~GHz, and a maximum turbo clock of 3.6~GHz.

\subsection{Statistical analysis of the results}
\label{subsec:bootstrap}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Given the amount of time available for this set of experiments, it was not
feasible to train multiple models under the same conditions. The variability
introduced by the stochastic training process is therefore not captured here. In
addition, eight hours of training is no guarantee of converged results. Both
limitations should be addressed in the following set of experiments, in which
repeated training runs per condition and a longer training schedule are proposed.

What can be quantified with the models at hand is the uncertainty on the
validation side. For each validation round, 200 episodes were run after training
and the per-episode KPIs were stored. From this sample, 95\% confidence intervals
were constructed using a statistical procedure called bootstrapping: the data
were randomly resampled with replacement into new datasets of equal size, and
this was repeated 10\,000 times to obtain an empirical distribution of each KPI.
The procedure follows Section~6.7 of \cite{FayGerow2013}.

Bootstrapping was chosen partly for its accessibility, but mainly because it
makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution of the data. This matters
here because several KPIs, such as the number of LoS events per flight hour, are
heavily skewed and bounded below by zero.
